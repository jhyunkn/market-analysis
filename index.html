Stock ratings: what the evidence says for a long-term and a short-term investor

—

Long-term (holding for years): every one of 8 business checks passes

Sales growing 8%+ a year for 3 years (and up in at least 2 of them), profitable each year, margins holding, manageable debt, high gross profit for its assets (top 40%; not used for banks, insurers, payment networks and property trusts, which get 7 checks), a reasonable price for the growth expected (PEG 2 or less), estimates rising, and most analysts rate it Buy. The price trend is not part of this rating.

Short-term (weeks to about 3 months): every one of 6 checks passes

In an uptrend, strong 12-month momentum, beat the S&P over the past month, earnings estimates rising, beat its last earnings report within about 10 weeks, and no report due in the next 10 days.

How the ratings work, and how far to trust them

Each check is a plain pass or fail on public numbers: fiscal-year sales, profit, operating margin and long-term debt from company filings; earnings surprises, consensus estimates and their revisions, and the forward PEG ratio from Nasdaq's analyst data (Zacks); price, trend, momentum and analyst ratings from this dashboard. Gross profitability (sales minus the direct cost of those sales, divided by total assets) is the quality measure with the strongest record in Novy-Marx's tests (2013); it is compared across these stocks, top 40% passes. A stock is marked cyclical when its operating margin swung more than 25 points over four years or it lost money in one of them: its fast growth may be a rebound from a bad year, which a 3-year growth figure cannot tell apart from steady growth. Strong means every check passes, Good all but one, Mixed at least half, Weak fewer than half. The ideas behind the checks have research support: stocks whose earnings estimates are being raised and that beat forecasts have tended to keep outperforming for a few months; momentum has a long record; profitable, growing companies have done better over long horizons. But this exact combination has not been tested here, the stock list holds only today's large companies, and this page's earlier test of its price-only checks found no edge. So every Strong list is logged weekly and graded against SPY after 1 and 3 months, shown above as it builds. These are rules applied the same way to every stock, not advice for your situation. A rating can change after any report or a few bad weeks; check the date.

    Scorecard: are these forward ranges right? Graded as each date arrives

    How to read

    Everything here looks forward. Option ranges come from what traders pay today for options that expire on a set date: the at-the-money call plus put (the straddle) prices how far the stock is expected to move by then, and the range shown is where it lands about 2 times in 3 if that price is fair. It is the market's own estimate, updated every build, not this page's opinion; option prices carry an insurance premium, so these ranges tend to run a little wide. The trend rule is a published, rules-only way to hold a fund: at each month-end close, stay in if the price is above its 10-month average, otherwise sit in Treasury bills. The switch line is the exact month-end close that would flip it. Its record here is over each fund's full history with no settings tuned. It has mostly cut the worst losses rather than added return. Recession odds are the New York Fed's yield-curve model for a recession starting within 12 months. GDPNow is the Atlanta Fed's running estimate of this quarter's growth. The scorecard logs every range this page shows and grades it when its date comes, so you can see whether to trust it. None of this predicts the direction of a price; it describes ranges, odds and risk states. Not investment advice.

    How to use this as a plan
    1. Choose a rule by the worst loss you could sit through, not by the highest past return. A rule you abandon halfway through a drawdown does worse than any of these. The worst drop and worst year columns are the ones to read.
    2. Check it once a month, after the close on the last trading day (this page rebuilds around 4:40 PM ET). Its "Holds now" column then shows what it holds for the next month.
    3. Trade only when the holdings change, and change them all at once. Between month-ends the rule does nothing, whatever the news.
    4. New money goes into whatever the rule holds now. The test below, and Vanguard's 2012 study, found investing at once usually came out ahead, because markets rise more often than they fall. Spreading it out or waiting mainly buys peace of mind, at a cost.
    5. Don't switch rules after a bad stretch. Every rule here has had years behind holding SPY; that is the price of its smaller losses. The live record below keeps score from now on.

    These are published rules shown with their full record, not recommendations. Which one fits depends on your goals, timeline, taxes and the losses you can tolerate, which this page cannot know. Past results, even from rules fixed in advance, do not guarantee future ones.

    How this is built

    23 FRED series, each judged against a fixed threshold written in advance (for example, core PCE above 3% counts as bad for stocks; a Baa corporate spread under 1.8 points counts as good). The regime chart groups every month since 1950 by its condition, using data already published that month, and shows the S&P 500's median return over the next year, measured against the median of all months (a normal year). Bars close to the dashed line mean the condition told you little. Hover a bar for the 1-in-10 bad year and sample size.

    How to read

    Percentile is the share of all past days with a lower reading; the bar runs from calm (left) to extreme stress (right). "Past days like today" are days whose reading sat within 5 percentile points of today's, and the S&P columns are its price change over the next 3 and 12 months after those days. Episodes count separate stretches (a gap of more than a month starts a new one): that is the real sample size. Medians are taken over days, so long sell-offs count more than short ones, which tends to make stressed setups look rosier than counting each episode once; fewer than 12 episodes is flagged as a small sample. Each gauge is read alone, gauges overlap heavily, and none of this has been tested as a timing rule. S&P price only, no dividends.

    How to read, and the limits

    Insider trades come from SEC Form 4 filings (filed within two business days). Open-market buys are the meaningful signal: research finds stocks bought by insiders, especially by several insiders at once, have tended to beat the market modestly over the following months. Sales are weaker evidence because insiders sell for many reasons, and pre-planned 10b5-1 sales are scheduled in advance, so they are shown separately; grants and option exercises are left out. Dollar values use today's price. Institutional changes come from 13F filings: positions as of the quarter end, filed up to 45 days later, long stock only (no shorts, and options are not netted), so they can be 2 to 4 months old. Hedge funds tracked: Berkshire, Bridgewater, Renaissance, Citadel, Millennium, Point72, Two Sigma, D. E. Shaw, Tiger Global, Coatue, Pershing Square, Viking, Lone Pine, Duquesne, Appaloosa, Third Point, Elliott, Soros, Scion, Baupost, Balyasny, Marshall Wace, Altimeter, Maverick, ARK, Tudor, Dragoneer, D1, Whale Rock, Greenlight and Icahn, found among each stock's 400 largest holders; Jane Street and Susquehanna are shown per stock as trading firms because their filings mostly reflect hedging. Short interest is reported by FINRA twice a month. Click any bar to open that stock. Analysis, not investment advice.

    What this is based on

    Where each part of the page comes from. None of it is a forecast; each has a known weakness, listed under it.

    • Your plan and the strategy labSeven published rules (each with its source on the page) run on month-end closes of funds with long histories (SPY since 1993, the others since 1998-2005), adjusted for dividends, with T-bills from FRED TB3MS and 0.10% per trade. Records are shown on one common period, by halves, against SPY over 3-year windows, and from each rule's publication date on, which is out of sample for its authors. The new-money test starts $1 at every month-end and measures it 24 months later. The tax view uses first-in-first-out lots and the rates you pick; dividends and interest are left out for every rule. Each month-end decision is logged and graded the next month.Seven rules is a small menu, chosen because they are well known, not because they are best. These fund histories hold two big bear markets (2008, 2020) and one rate shock (2022); a different kind of crisis could behave differently. None of the rules beat holding SPY on return.
    • PricesDaily closes from Yahoo Finance since 2017 for the stock analysis, adjusted for splits and dividends, through the latest completed market close.This window is one long bull market with a few sharp breaks, so "normal" here reflects a generally favorable era.
    • Likely and possible rangesEach stock's own volatility: today's level (exponentially weighted, recent days count most) fading toward its 1-year average with a 3-month half-life. Bands are 2-in-3 and 9-in-10 outcomes with no up or down drift.Real moves have fatter tails than these bands; a crash can land outside the light band.
    • If the S&P repeats historyThe median S&P 500 path over the year after past sell-offs like today (VIX 30 or higher with the S&P 8–20% below its high, since 1990, each episode counted once), scaled by the stock's trailing five-year beta to SPY. Newer listings use all available post-IPO history. Leveraged funds instead compound the underlying's path day by day: price x (underlying path)^3 x exp(-3 x the underlying's cumulative variance) x financing and fees (2 x the 3-month T-bill plus 0.95% a year).A median across few episodes; any single year can be far from it, and beta drifts.
    • Setups like this, for each stockPast days after the first one-year warm-up when the stock sat within 5 points of today's distance below its 52-week high and on the same side of its 200-day average. Matched days bunch together, so each episode (matches less than a month apart) counts once, from its first day; episodes whose 12-month windows overlap are merged into one cluster and averaged. The rate is the share of clusters higher 12 months later, against non-overlapping 12-month windows on the same history. Fewer than 8 independent clusters shows "insufficient sample" and is not scored.With prices since 2017, few stocks reach 8 clusters, so this check is usually not scored. Survivorship: these are stocks that are big today.
    • Analyst targetsConsensus from Nasdaq's public analyst data, refreshed with every data build: buy/hold/sell counts and the low, average and high 12-month target, with the date it last changed when available. A stock whose targets have not refreshed for 45 days is marked stale and not scored.Targets lag prices; many were set before this sell-off and tend to be optimistic.
    • Evidence state and levelsFour checks score the stock: trend (price vs 200-day, 50-day slope), setups-like-this edge of 10+ points over its normal year, the S&P history path lifting it 5%+, and a reachable analyst target with 70%+ buy ratings. "Confirms above" is the nearer moving average above the price (or the 1-month high when above both). "Nearest support" is the main bottom zone on the bottom map. Leveraged funds (TQQQ on QQQ, UPRO on SPY, TECL on XLK, SOXL on SOXX; see config/leveraged.json): zones and the confirmation level are built on the underlying and translated as fund price x (1 + 3 x the underlying's move), a one-session estimate recalculated daily; their "Weak checks" state rests on conditions visible today (underlying downtrend, volatility 1.4x normal or more, unsettled credit) and names the ones that fired; crash replay covers only the fund's own life, and a separate simulated stress test covers 2000 and 2008.Rules written in advance. Walk-forward tests show no edge so far: the trend and setups-like-this checks did not separate 3-month winners from losers (see the track record above the ranking), and the bottom zones held no more often than random levels (see the bottom map).
